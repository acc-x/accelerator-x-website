# Newsletter versions of the three bootcamp drafts

Drafted 21 November 2026 for founder review. Not wired into Brevo or anything else, and not sent. Each is 120 to 180 words and links to its full article once that is published.

---

## 1. Give AI a desk, not a question (Andy)

Ask leaders what frustrates them about AI and two answers keep coming up: the output needs reworking, and the tool does not understand the business.

Both have the same cause. Most of us use AI as a question box, so every chat starts from zero and we pay for the missing context every time.

The fix is to give it a desk. Tell it once who you are and how you work; every mainstream assistant has a settings box for this. Give each recurring job its own folder with inputs, outputs and reference inside, plus a short rules file saying what the folder is for and what it must never do. Then the prompt only has to carry what is new today.

This week: spend thirty minutes writing your standing instructions, make one folder for one monthly job, and run a real task in it. Count how much you no longer had to explain.

Full article, with a prompt you can copy: [Give AI a desk, not a question](/insights/articles/give-ai-a-desk-not-a-question.html)

## 2. Anything you have explained to AI twice is a job worth saving (Andy)

In a recent session of our current cohort, everyone ran the same request on the same meeting notes. What came back varied a lot: bullets for some, tables for others, actions and owners all over the place. The request left the decisions to the assistant, so it made them afresh for each person.

The same thing happens to you across weeks. A slightly different request for the Monday update gives a slightly different update, each needing its own fixes.

So the second time you explain a job, save the method instead: what goes in, what comes out, the order, a past example of good, and a final check of every number and name against the source. When the result is wrong, change the method, not the output, and every later run improves.

This week: pick the job you explain most often, write its method in half a page, and run it twice on real material.

Full article, with an example method: [Anything you have explained to AI twice is a job worth saving](/insights/articles/anything-you-explain-to-ai-twice-is-a-job-to-save.html)

## 3. Check the file, not the reply (Toby)

In the second week of our current cohort, a leader's AI assistant told them it had saved a document. It hadn't, and when asked again, it said so.

Anyone who has run a team knows the pattern from a status report marked "complete". The rule that fixes it is the oldest one in delegation: before you rely on the report, look at the thing.

Three checks, a minute or two each: ask the assistant to read back what it saved; open the file yourself; start a fresh conversation and see whether it can find it unaided. Then write your boundary into its standing instructions: everything it makes is a draft, external tools are read-only until you decide otherwise, and it always says what it could not check.

This week: run the three checks on the AI output you rely on most, and add one line to your instructions telling it never to claim something is saved or sent unless it has confirmed it.

Full article: [Check the file, not the reply](/insights/articles/check-the-file-not-the-reply.html)
