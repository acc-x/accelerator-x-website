Anything You Have Explained to AI Twice Is a Job Worth Saving

1 December 2026

Andy Carroll

Co-founder

Andy is a product and AI specialist with 17+ years across startups, banking, and consultancies.

bolt

Executive Summary

If you have explained the same job to an AI assistant twice, stop retyping it and save the method: what goes in, what comes out, how to get from one to the other, an example of good, and a check against the source. When the result is wrong, change the method rather than the output, and every later run improves.

Why does the same AI request give such different results?

In a recent session of our current bootcamp cohort, everyone in the room ran the same request on the same set of made-up meeting notes: turn these into a clear write-up. Same notes, same words, same tool. What came back varied a lot. Some people got tidy bullet points, some got tables, and the actions and owners came out differently from screen to screen.

Nobody had done anything wrong. The request simply left most of the decisions to the assistant, so it made them afresh for each person. It was a more convincing demonstration than any slide we could have shown, because the same thing happens to every leader using AI on their own, just spread across weeks instead of across a room. On Monday you type a careful paragraph about the weekly update. The following Monday you are busier and type two lines. The week after, you paste last week's version and say "same again". Three requests, three slightly different results, three different sets of fixes.

What should you do instead?

Notice the second time you explain a job, and save the method rather than typing it a third time.

A saved method is a short, written-down way of doing one recurring job, which the assistant follows every time you ask for that job. Claude calls these skills. In ChatGPT the nearest equivalent is a custom GPT. Whatever your tool calls it, the idea is the same: the decisions you keep making in your head (what goes in, what order, how long, what to leave out, what to check) get made once, on paper, and stay made.

It does not need any technical skill. If you can brief a capable new member of staff, you can write one.

What does one look like?

Here is an illustrative example for a weekly team update. The business is made up; the point is the level of detail, which is less than most people expect.

Monday team update. Each Monday I give you last week's sales and delivery numbers and a few lines of rough notes. Turn them into an update I can send to my senior team. Start with what changed most since last week and why it matters. Then the numbers, as a short table against the previous week. Then anything I need the team to decide, each as a question with a name next to it. Finish with the two or three things that matter most this week. Keep it to one screen. Write the way I talk to my team: direct, no jargon, no cheerleading. Never make up a figure. If a number is missing, write [missing] and tell me. Before you hand it over, check every number and name against what I gave you, and list anything you could not match. Here is one I wrote myself last month, so you can see what good looks like: [paste a past update, with anything sensitive removed].

Two parts of that do most of the work. The past example shows your standard far better than any description of "short and clear" can. And the final check is what stops a fluent, confident draft from going out with a figure or a name quietly wrong.

What happens when it gets something wrong?

Fix the method, not the output. If the update comes back with the decisions buried at the bottom, do not just move them and send it. Change the method so they come first, then run it again. Patching the output fixes this week. Patching the method fixes every week after it. It is the same principle we wrote about in Before You Build Anything, Get the Plan Torn Apart: fix the system that produced the result, not just the result.

That is where the return comes from. A saved method gets a little better every time you correct it, and because it is written down it can be handed to a colleague, so a job is done the same way across the team rather than five different ways. That is how the same people end up getting through considerably more, rather than the same work being done with fewer of them.

How do you choose the first one?

Pick something boring that comes round every week and has an obvious finished product. Ambition can wait; you want a method you will run often enough to improve quickly. Keep it to drafting: the assistant prepares, you check and send.

What can you do this week?

  1. Write down three things you have explained to AI, or to a person, more than once this month.
  2. Pick the one you do most often and write its method in the shape above, including a real past example with anything sensitive taken out.
  3. Run it twice on real material before Friday. Each time something is wrong, change the method, then run it again.
